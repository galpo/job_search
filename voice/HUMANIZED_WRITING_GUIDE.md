# Humanized Writing Guide

Craft reference for what makes writing read as human, not AI-generated or corporate. Applies to Substack posts, LinkedIn posts, cover letters, interview answers, and any long-form narrative content. (Outreach DMs stay bullet-format per `outreach/TEMPLATES.md` — but individual sentences inside them should still follow the specificity and honesty principles below instead of defaulting to corporate phrasing.)

## Reference passage (external — style model only, not to be reused as content)

> "Don't be evil" mantra. While it came out of a corporate values exercise, in which Paul Bucheit coined the phrase to replace a laundry list of things we believed in, the interpretation was always up to individual employees. I think for Larry, the biggest meaning was that we would do nothing to waste users' time or diminish the quality of our algorithmically derived search results. The connection he made to how employees were treated was more tenuous.
>
> Larry was ruthlessly efficient in his treatment of staff, as when he fired all the project managers because he felt they were getting in the way of engineers doing exactly what he wanted. I suspect his calculation in the Rubin severance package was largely devoid of consideration for how staff would feel about it.
>
> If you look at Google's history, the executives have never been particularly attuned to social engagement. They built Google Plus and loaded it with engineering features to compete with Facebook, but failed miserably to make it attractive to "normal" people. Larry barely engaged with it and I'm sure he scorned all the cat pictures and food photos that Facebook users posted on the way to making it a success. And while Sergey was more of an extrovert, he set a tone that didn't recognize boundaries between management and staff, which created a permissive environment that others exploited.
>
> While I haven't yet read Claire Stapleton's book, I suspect the seeds of the issues she raises were planted at the company's founding, by the founders themselves. I'm sorry to learn how widespread the rot has spread. If I were to write my book now, I would put a less positive spin on the behaviors that at the time seemed harmless, if juvenile. I'm glad that Claire's book promises to offer a more balanced view.

## Why this reads as human

1. **Opinions are owned, not asserted.** "I think," "I suspect," "I'm sorry to learn" — these mark a claim about someone else's motives as the writer's own read, not a fact. That's honest, not weak. Contrast with corporate hedges ("it's worth noting," "arguably"), which soften a claim without taking ownership of it.
2. **Concrete, named details carry the argument.** Paul Bucheit, the Rubin severance package, Google Plus, cat pictures and food photos. Specific nouns do the work that adjectives and category-words ("initiatives," "stakeholders," "engagement strategies") only gesture at.
3. **Contradiction is allowed to stand.** Larry is "ruthlessly efficient" and specifically right about search quality in the same breath. Sergey is "more of an extrovert" and the cause of a "permissive environment that others exploited." Neither gets flattened into a villain or a hero — the write-up trusts the reader to hold both.
4. **The writer revises their own past take, in public.** "If I were to write my book now, I would put a less positive spin on the behaviors that at the time seemed harmless." Updating your own prior judgment out loud builds more trust in one sentence than any amount of defending it would.
5. **No throat-clearing, no forced conclusion.** It starts mid-thought and ends on a plain, proportionate reaction ("I'm glad that Claire's book promises to offer a more balanced view") — not a summary, not a call to action, not an uplift tacked on because the piece needs to "land."
6. **Sentences do one job each; length varies naturally.** Short declaratives sit next to longer ones with a subordinate clause or two. Nothing is padded to sound more substantial.

## Do

- Lead with a specific, named detail instead of a general claim.
- State a read on someone else's motive as your own perception ("I think," "I suspect") rather than as settled fact.
- Let a person or situation stay contradictory instead of resolving it into a tidy verdict.
- Revise or soften a past claim of your own when it's genuinely warranted — say so plainly.
- Use plain nouns for concrete things (a title, a product, a person's job) instead of category words.
- Vary sentence length; let a short sentence land after a long one.
- End on a plain, proportionate reaction, not a summary or a pitch.

## Avoid

- Hedge-everything corporate qualifiers: "it's worth noting that," "arguably," "in many ways," "needless to say."
- Superlatives and marketing adjectives: "incredible," "game-changing," "world-class," "cutting-edge."
- Transition scaffolding: "Furthermore," "In conclusion," "That said," "At the end of the day."
- Flattening a person or company into a one-note villain or hero.
- Closing with a forced positive spin or a CTA when the moment doesn't call for one.
- Em dashes (existing rule — use semicolons, colons, or commas instead).

## Self-edit checklist before finalizing any narrative draft

- [ ] Did I replace at least one vague claim with a specific, named detail?
- [ ] Does every "I think / I believe" mark an actual opinion, not a hedge to avoid commitment?
- [ ] Is there a corporate qualifier or transition word I can just delete?
- [ ] Would a reader believe a real person wrote this, contradictions and all?
- [ ] Does it end on a plain reaction instead of a wrap-up sentence or a pitch?
- [ ] No em dashes.
